Gear doesn't make a studio business. Bookings do. The engineers in Howard Massey's *Behind the Glass* agree on very little about technique, but they keep returning to one point: a great engineer on modest gear beats a mediocre one on a famous console.

## Buy in this order

1. **Monitoring.** You can't fix what you can't hear. Mike Senior's advice in *Mixing Secrets for the Small Studio*: put as much of your budget as possible into accurate studio monitors (active, not hi-fi), then learn them.
2. **Treatment, about as much as you spent on speakers.** Senior's baseline for most small rooms is about a dozen thick mineral-fibre bass traps with an air gap behind them, plus around 4–5 m² of 10 cm foam at the reflection points. Bass is the hard part in small rooms, and EQ can't fix room modes.
3. **Checking systems.** One small single speaker in mono, a top set of headphones, and a cheap "worst case" speaker.
4. **One excellent microphone,** then good-enough mics from reputable makers. You mostly record one source at a time.
5. **Everything else,** only when a paying client needs it.

## Phase it, with gates

| Phase | What you buy | Gate before the next phase |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Validate | Almost nothing; rent time elsewhere | Strangers pay you, and come back |
| 2. Hear the truth | Monitors, mono speaker, headphones, DIY traps and foam, measurement mic | Clients need something your room can't do |
| 3. Engineer it | Noise measurement, permits, structural review, landlord consent | Pre-sold hours cover the build within the lease |
| 4. Build and tune | Isolation construction, quiet HVAC, clean power | (a one-way door) |

Phases 1 and 2 are cheap and reversible, and they're enough for release-quality mixing.

## Payback: the only gear question that matters

Gear payback = cost ÷ extra monthly profit it brings in. If you can't name the extra bookings a purchase wins, it's a hobby purchase. That's fine, but pay for it from your own pay, not the studio's.

**Example (estimates):** a $60,000 build-out that lets you take 40 more band-tracking hours a month at $60 brings in $2,400 a month. That's **25 months to pay back** before engineer time, power and wear. On a 3-year lease where improvements belong to the landlord, that's thin. Pre-sell the tracking hours first. Try your own numbers in the [gear payback tool](../../tools/gear-payback/).

## Buy, rent or finance?

- **Rent** specialty gear for the session that needs it, and bill it to the client.
- **Buy used** from reputable sellers. Good mics and monitors hold their value. Computers and interfaces don't.
- **Finance** only gear that is already earning, and keep payments inside your operating-expenses limit (about 30% of real revenue, from [Money](../money/)).
- **Save first:** Profit First suggests a "major equipment" account. Divide the target cost by the months until you'll buy, halve it, and move that amount every 10th and 25th.

## Myths that waste money

- Egg crates, carpet on walls and mattresses absorb almost nothing in the bass range, where small rooms need it most. They're also fire risks.
- Products sold as "soundproofing" rarely beat plain 5/8" fire-rated drywall per dollar.
- Expensive plugins don't fix a bad balance. Senior's view: low-budget processing is no excuse for a bad mix.

## Checklist

- [ ] I've named the one thing my room will be known for
- [ ] My monitoring and treatment budget is set before mics and outboard
- [ ] I've measured my room before treatment, and I'll measure again after
- [ ] I have a mono check speaker, good headphones and a worst-case speaker in the plan
- [ ] Every planned purchase over a few hundred dollars has a payback calculation
- [ ] I've listed gear I can rent per session instead of buying
- [ ] Big purchases come from a savings account, not from a busy month
