Most studios don't close because the acoustics are bad. They close because the calendar is empty and the rent is due. The cure starts here, before any gear or space: decide exactly who you're for.

## Start with the life, not the room

Before you plan a business, decide what it's for. How many nights a week do you want to work? How much do you need to earn? What creative work do you still want time for? Michael Gerber calls this your *primary aim*, and it sets the size of studio worth building. A one-room vocal studio that pays you well four days a week is a success. A three-room facility that eats every weekend may not be.

## Find a niche you can explain to a stranger

A niche is a clear answer to "who books you, and why you?" Good niches are specific enough that the right people recognize themselves straight away:

- "Release-ready vocals for singer-songwriters across Lanark County, without driving to Ottawa."
- "I record singer-songwriters live, in one room, in a day."
- "Podcasts for Perth-area businesses and nonprofits, recorded and edited for you."
- "Rehearsal lockouts for working cover bands."

Write yours with **no adjectives**. "Amazing", "professional" and "high-quality" say nothing a competitor can't also say. If you're tempted to use one, ask *why*, and use the answer instead.

## Three questions that test a niche

1. **What can you do that a bedroom can't?** A good live room, a proven vocal chain, real production feedback, a calm space, video, or your ears and credits.
2. **What process are you proud enough to film?** If you wouldn't show it, it isn't your edge. (You'll use this in [Marketing](../marketing/).)
3. **Who's the self-funded client who wants it?** Most independent artists pay for their own records now. That's your core market, along with businesses that want podcasts.

## Pick a who-where segment

From *The Mom Test*: a useful segment is a **type of person plus a place you can reliably find them**. "Musicians in Lanark County" fails. "Folk and roots singer-songwriters who play open mics around Perth and want to release an EP" works, because you know where to find them this week.

If conversations keep giving you contradicting answers, your segment is too broad. Narrow it.

## Plan for a small-town market

Perth is a small town, so the local pool of paying clients is smaller than in a city. Plan your reach from the start:

- **Draw from the whole region:** Lanark County, Smiths Falls and Carleton Place, plus Ottawa and Kingston artists who would drive for a quieter setting, a lower rate or a specific sound.
- **Add work that doesn't need clients in the room:** remote mixing and podcast editing let you serve clients anywhere.
- **Serve local businesses:** downtown shops, tourism businesses and nonprofits are natural podcast and audio clients, and they're reachable through the local Chamber and BIA (see [Marketing](../marketing/)).

## Recording, podcast or both?

Each model makes money differently and breaks in a different place:

| Model | Main income | Watch this number | Biggest trap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Recording studio | Hourly, day and block bookings; production and mixing | Utilization (booked hours ÷ available hours) | A big lease before steady bookings |
| Podcast room rental | Hourly room and video packages | Bookings per week; repeat clients | Competing on price with no difference |
| Podcast production | Monthly retainers per show | Gross margin per show; churn | Underpricing editing time |
| Mixing and production | Per-song fees, points | Revenue per client; repeat rate | Working for "exposure" or unsigned splits |
| Rehearsal space | Monthly lockouts, hourly rooms | Occupancy | Noise complaints and zoning |

You can stack these later. Start with the one your skills and network already support.
